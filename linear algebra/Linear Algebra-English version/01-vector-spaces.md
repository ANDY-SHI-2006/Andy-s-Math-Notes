# 1. Vector Spaces

## 1.1 Fields

### 1.1.1 Definition

Let $K \subset \mathbf{C}$. $K$ is a **field** if:

| Condition        | Statement                                                          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **(a) Closure**  | If $x, y \in K$, then $x + y \in K$ and $xy \in K$.                |
| **(b) Inverses** | If $x \in K$, then $-x \in K$; if $x \neq 0$, then $x^{-1} \in K$. |
| **(c) Identity** | $0 \in K$ and $1 \in K$.                                           |

> **Key idea:** A field is a set where addition, multiplication, and division by non-zero elements obey the ordinary rules of arithmetic.

### 1.1.2 Examples

| Set | Field? | Reason |
|-----|--------|--------|
| $\mathbf{R}$ (real numbers) | Yes | |
| $\mathbf{C}$ (complex numbers) | Yes | |
| $\mathbf{Q}$ (rational numbers) | Yes | |
| $\mathbf{Z}$ (integers) | **No** | Fails (b): e.g., $2^{-1} = \frac{1}{2} \notin \mathbf{Z}$. |

**Subfield:** If $K \subset L$ and both are fields, $K$ is a **subfield** of $L$.
- $\mathbf{R}$ is a subfield of $\mathbf{C}$; $\mathbf{Q}$ is a subfield of $\mathbf{R}$.

Elements of a field are called **numbers** or **scalars**.

## 1.2 Vector Spaces

### 1.2.1 Definition

A **vector space** $V$ over a field $K$ is a set of objects that can be **added** and **multiplied by scalars** (elements of $K$), satisfying:

| Axiom | Statement |
|-------|-----------|
| **VS 1.** Associativity | $(u + v) + w = u + (v + w)$ |
| **VS 2.** Zero element | There exists $O \in V$ such that $O + u = u + O = u$ for all $u \in V$. |
| **VS 3.** Additive inverse | For each $u \in V$, there exists $-u \in V$ such that $u + (-u) = O$. |
| **VS 4.** Commutativity | $u + v = v + u$ |
| **VS 5.** Distributivity (scalar over vector sum) | $c(u + v) = cu + cv$ |
| **VS 6.** Distributivity (scalar sum over vector) | $(a + b)v = av + bv$ |
| **VS 7.** Compatibility | $(ab)v = a(bv)$ |
| **VS 8.** Identity | $1 \cdot u = u$ |

> **Note:** The zero element $O$ is uniquely determined by VS 2.

**Notation:**
- $0$ = number zero; $O$ = zero vector.
- $u - v$ means $u + (-v)$.

### 1.2.2 Basic Properties

**Property.** $0v = O$ for any $v \in V$.

**Proof:** $0v + v = 0v + 1v = (0 + 1)v = 1v = v$. Adding $-v$ to both sides gives $0v = O$.

### 1.2.3 Example — $K^n$

Let $V = K^n$ = set of $n$-tuples of elements of $K$.

For $A = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ in $K^n$:

$$
A + B = (a_1 + b_1, \dots, a_n + b_n), \qquad cA = (ca_1, \dots, ca_n) \; (c \in K).
$$

The zero element is $O = (0, \dots, 0)$. All axioms VS 1–VS 8 are satisfied.

- $\mathbf{C}^n$ is a vector space over $\mathbf{C}$; $\mathbf{Q}^n$ is a vector space over $\mathbf{Q}$.
- $\mathbf{R}^n$ is **not** a vector space over $\mathbf{C}$.

> **Convention:** When we write $K^n$, it is always understood as a vector space over $K$. Elements of $K^n$ (and of any vector space) are called **vectors**.

## 1.3 Subspaces

### 1.3.1 Definition

Let $V$ be a vector space and $W \subset V$. $W$ is a **subspace** of $V$ if:

| Condition | Statement |
|-----------|-----------|
| **(i)** Closure under addition | If $v, w \in W$, then $v + w \in W$. |
| **(ii)** Closure under scalar multiplication | If $v \in W$ and $c \in K$, then $cv \in W$. |
| **(iii)** Contains zero | $O \in W$. |

Then $W$ itself is a vector space (axioms VS 1–VS 8 hold in $W$ because they hold in $V$).

### 1.3.2 Example — $K^{n-1}$ in $K^n$

Let $V = K^n$ and let $W$ be the set of vectors whose last coordinate is $0$. Then $W$ is a subspace of $V$, identifiable with $K^{n-1}$.

### 1.3.3 Intersection of Subspaces

Let $U, W$ be subspaces of $V$. Then $U \cap W$ is a **subspace** of $V$.

> **Geometric intuition:** If $U, W$ are two planes through the origin in 3-space, their intersection is generally a straight line through the origin.

### 1.3.4 Sum of Subspaces

Let $U, W$ be subspaces of $V$. The **sum** $U + W$ is the set of all elements $u + w$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in W$. Then $U + W$ is a **subspace** of $V$, said to be generated by $U$ and $W$.

## 1.4 Linear Combinations

### 1.4.1 Definition

Let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in V$ and $x_1, \dots, x_n \in K$. An expression of the form:

$$
x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_n v_n
$$

is called a **linear combination** of $v_1, \dots, v_n$.

### 1.4.2 Generated Subspaces

Let $W$ be the set of all linear combinations of $v_1, \dots, v_n$. Then $W$ is a **subspace** of $V$.

**Proof sketch:**
- Sum of two linear combinations is a linear combination.
- Scalar multiple of a linear combination is a linear combination.
- $O = 0v_1 + \cdots + 0v_n \in W$.

This subspace $W$ is called the subspace **generated by** $v_1, \dots, v_n$. If $W = V$, we say $v_1, \dots, v_n$ **generate** $V$.

## 1.5 Dot Product

### 1.5.1 Definition

For $A = (a_1, \dots, a_n)$ and $B = (b_1, \dots, b_n)$ in $K^n$, the **dot product** (or **scalar product**) is:

$$
A \cdot B = a_1 b_1 + \cdots + a_n b_n.
$$

### 1.5.2 Properties

| Property | Statement |
|----------|-----------|
| **SP 1.** Commutativity | $A \cdot B = B \cdot A$ |
| **SP 2.** Distributivity | $A \cdot (B + C) = A \cdot B + A \cdot C = (B + C) \cdot A$ |
| **SP 3.** Homogeneity | $(xA) \cdot B = x(A \cdot B)$ and $A \cdot (xB) = x(A \cdot B)$ |

**Additional identities:**

$$
(A + B)^2 = A^2 + 2A \cdot B + B^2, \qquad (A - B)^2 = A^2 - 2A \cdot B + B^2
$$

where $A^2 = A \cdot A$.

### 1.5.3 Orthogonality

$A$ and $B$ are **perpendicular** (or **orthogonal**) if $A \cdot B = 0$.

> **Note:** $A \cdot B = 0$ does not imply $A = O$ or $B = O$. Example: $A = (1, 2, 3)$, $B = (2, 1, -\frac{4}{3})$, then $A \cdot B = 0$.

Let $W$ be the set of all $B \in K^n$ such that $B \cdot A = 0$ (fixed $A$). Then $W$ is a **subspace** of $K^n$.

## 1.6 Function Spaces

### 1.6.1 Definition

Let $S$ be a set and $K$ a field. A **function** $f: S \to K$ assigns to each element of $S$ a unique element of $K$.

Let $V$ be the set of all functions from $S$ into $K$. Define:
- $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$
- $(cf)(x) = cf(x)$ for $c \in K$

Then $V$ is a vector space over $K$. The zero element is the **zero function** $0$ (where $0(x) = 0$ for all $x \in S$).

### 1.6.2 Subspaces

| Subspace | Condition |
|----------|-----------|
| Continuous functions | $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ continuous |
| Differentiable functions | $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ differentiable (subspace of continuous functions) |

**Example:** Let $f(t) = e^t$ and $g(t) = e^{2t}$. These generate a subspace of differentiable functions; $3e^t + 2e^{2t}$ and $2e^t + \pi e^{2t}$ are elements of this subspace.

## 1.7 Bases

### 1.7.1 Linear Dependence and Independence

Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_n \in V$.

- $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are **linearly dependent** over $K$ if there exist $a_1, \dots, a_n \in K$, **not all zero**, such that:

$$
a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_n v_n = O.
$$

- $v_1, \dots, v_n$ are **linearly independent** if no such numbers exist. Equivalently:

> Whenever $a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_n v_n = O$, then $a_i = 0$ for all $i = 1, \dots, n$.

**Example 1:** In $V = K^n$, the vectors $E_1 = (1, 0, \dots, 0), \dots, E_n = (0, \dots, 1)$ are linearly independent, since $a_1 E_1 + \cdots + a_n E_n = (a_1, \dots, a_n) = O$ implies all $a_i = 0$.

**Example 2:** The functions $e^t, e^{2t}$ are linearly independent.

**Proof:** Suppose $ae^t + be^{2t} = 0$ for all $t$. Differentiating: $ae^t + 2be^{2t} = 0$. Subtracting: $be^{2t} = 0 \implies b = 0$. Then $ae^t = 0 \implies a = 0$.

### 1.7.2 Definition of a Basis

If $v_1, \dots, v_n$ **generate** $V$ and are **linearly independent**, then $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is called a **basis** of $V$.

- $E_1, \dots, E_n$ form a basis of $K^n$.
- $\{e^t, e^{2t}\}$ is a basis of the subspace they generate.

### 1.7.3 Coordinates

**Theorem 2.1.** Let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be linearly independent. If:

$$
x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_n v_n = y_1 v_1 + \cdots + y_n v_n,
$$

then $x_i = y_i$ for all $i$.

**Proof:** Subtracting gives $(x_1 - y_1)v_1 + \cdots + (x_n - y_n)v_n = O$. By linear independence, $x_i - y_i = 0$.

Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ be a basis of $V$. Any $v \in V$ can be written uniquely as:

$$
v = x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_n v_n.
$$

The $n$-tuple $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is called the **coordinates** of $v$ with respect to this basis; $x_i$ is the **$i$-th coordinate**. The $n$-tuple $X = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$ is the **coordinate vector** of $v$.

**Example 3:** The coordinates of $3e^t + 5e^{2t}$ with respect to $\{e^t, e^{2t}\}$ are $(3, 5)$.

**Example 4:** Show that $(1, 1)$ and $(-3, 2)$ are linearly independent.

Suppose $a(1, 1) + b(-3, 2) = O$. Then $a - 3b = 0$ and $a + 2b = 0$. Subtracting: $-5b = 0 \implies b = 0$, then $a = 0$.

**Example 5:** Find the coordinates of $(1, 0)$ with respect to the basis $\{(1, 1), (-1, 2)\}$.

Solve $a(1, 1) + b(-1, 2) = (1, 0)$:
- $a - b = 1$, $a + 2b = 0$
- $3b = -1 \implies b = -\frac{1}{3}$, $a = \frac{2}{3}$

Coordinates: $\left(\frac{2}{3}, -\frac{1}{3}\right)$.

**Example 6:** Show that $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, 2)$ form a basis of $\mathbf{R}^2$.

- **Linear independence:** $a(1, 1) + b(-1, 2) = (0, 0) \implies a = b = 0$.
- **Generate $\mathbf{R}^2$:** For any $(a, b) \in \mathbf{R}^2$, solve $x(1, 1) + y(-1, 2) = (a, b)$:
  - $x = \frac{2a + b}{3}$, $y = \frac{b - a}{3}$

### 1.7.4 Maximal Subsets of Linearly Independent Elements

Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\} \subset V$ and $r \le n$. $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ is a **maximal subset of linearly independent elements** if:
1. $v_1, \dots, v_r$ are linearly independent.
2. For any $v_i$ with $i > r$, the set $\{v_1, \dots, v_r, v_i\}$ is linearly dependent.

**Theorem 2.2.** Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ generate $V$. Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ be a maximal subset of linearly independent elements. Then $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ is a **basis** of $V$.

**Proof:** We must show $v_1, \dots, v_r$ generate $V$.

1. For $i > r$: since $\{v_1, \dots, v_r, v_i\}$ is dependent, there exist $x_1, \dots, x_r, y$ not all $0$ with:
   $$
   x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_r v_r + y v_i = O.
   $$
   Since $v_1, \dots, v_r$ are independent, $y \neq 0$. Hence:
   $$
   v_i = \frac{x_1}{-y} v_1 + \cdots + \frac{x_r}{-y} v_r.
   $$

2. For any $v \in V$: $v = c_1 v_1 + \cdots + c_n v_n$. Replace each $v_i$ ($i > r$) by a linear combination of $v_1, \dots, v_r$. Collecting terms expresses $v$ as a linear combination of $v_1, \dots, v_r$.

## 1.8 Dimension of a Vector Space

### 1.8.1 Theorem 3.1

**Theorem 3.1.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$. Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}$ be a basis of $V$. Let $w_1, \dots, w_n \in V$ with $n > m$. Then $w_1, \dots, w_n$ are linearly dependent.

**Proof sketch (Steinitz exchange):**

1. Since $\{v_1, \dots, v_m\}$ is a basis:
   $$
   w_1 = a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_m v_m.
   $$
   Since $w_1 \neq O$, some $a_i \neq 0$. Assume $a_1 \neq 0$. Then:
   $$
   v_1 = a_1^{-1} w_1 - a_1^{-1} a_2 v_2 - \cdots - a_1^{-1} a_m v_m.
   $$
   Thus $\{w_1, v_2, \dots, v_m\}$ generates $V$.

2. By induction: assume $\{w_1, \dots, w_r, v_{r+1}, \dots, v_m\}$ generates $V$. Then:
   $$
   w_{r+1} = b_1 w_1 + \cdots + b_r w_r + c_{r+1} v_{r+1} + \cdots + c_m v_m.
   $$
   Some $c_j \neq 0$ (otherwise $w_1, \dots, w_{r+1}$ dependent). Assume $c_{r+1} \neq 0$. Then $v_{r+1}$ is a linear combination of $w_1, \dots, w_{r+1}, v_{r+2}, \dots, v_m$.

3. By induction, $\{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$ generates $V$. If $n > m$:
   $$
   w_n = d_1 w_1 + \cdots + d_m w_m,
   $$
   proving $w_1, \dots, w_n$ are dependent.

### 1.8.2 Theorem 3.2 — Uniqueness of Dimension

**Theorem 3.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space. If one basis has $n$ elements and another has $m$ elements, then $m = n$.

**Proof:** Apply Theorem 3.1 to both bases. $n > m$ and $m > n$ are both impossible.

### 1.8.3 Definition of Dimension

Let $V$ have a basis of $n$ elements. Then $n$ is the **dimension** of $V$, denoted $\dim_K V$ or $\dim V$.

- If $V = \{O\}$, then $\dim V = 0$ (no basis).

A vector space with a finite basis (or the zero space) is **finite dimensional**; otherwise **infinite dimensional**.

> **Convention:** In this book, "dimension" always means finite dimensional.

**Example 1:** $\mathbf{R}^n$ has dimension $n$ over $\mathbf{R}$; $\mathbf{C}^n$ has dimension $n$ over $\mathbf{C}$; $K^n$ has dimension $n$ over $K$.

**Example 2:** $K$ is a vector space over itself of dimension $1$. The element $1$ forms a basis, since any $x \in K$ has $x = x \cdot 1$.

**Example 3:** A subspace of dimension $1$ is called a **line** in $V$. A subspace of dimension $2$ is called a **plane** in $V$.

### 1.8.4 Maximal Sets of Linearly Independent Elements

Let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be linearly independent. They form a **maximal set of linearly independent elements** of $V$ if for any $w \in V$, the elements $w, v_1, \dots, v_n$ are linearly dependent.

**Theorem 3.3.** Let $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ be a maximal set of linearly independent elements of $V$. Then $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is a basis of $V$.

**Proof:** For any $w \in V$: $w, v_1, \dots, v_n$ are dependent, so there exist $x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n$ not all $0$ with:
$$
x_0 w + x_1 v_1 + \cdots + x_n v_n = O.
$$
We cannot have $x_0 = 0$ (that would make $v_1, \dots, v_n$ dependent). Hence:
$$
w = -\frac{x_1}{x_0} v_1 - \cdots - \frac{x_n}{x_0} v_n.
$$
Thus $v_1, \dots, v_n$ generate $V$.

### 1.8.5 Theorem 3.4 — Linearly Independent Elements Form a Basis

**Theorem 3.4.** Let $\dim V = n$, and let $v_1, \dots, v_n$ be linearly independent. Then $v_1, \dots, v_n$ constitute a basis of $V$.

**Proof:** By Theorem 3.1, $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is a maximal set of linearly independent elements. By Theorem 3.3, it is a basis.

### 1.8.6 Corollaries

**Corollary 3.5.** Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$. If $\dim W = \dim V$ then $W = V$.

**Proof:** A basis for $W$ is also a basis for $V$ by Theorem 3.4.

**Corollary 3.6.** Let $\dim V = n$, $r < n$, and $v_1, \dots, v_r$ be linearly independent. Then one can find $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ such that $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ is a basis of $V$.

**Proof:** Since $r < n$, $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ is not maximal. Find $v_{r+1}$ such that $v_1, \dots, v_{r+1}$ are independent. Repeat until $n$ independent elements are obtained. By Theorem 3.4, this is a basis.

### 1.8.7 Theorem 3.7 — Dimension of a Subspace

**Theorem 3.7.** Let $\dim V = n$ and $W$ be a subspace not consisting of $O$ alone. Then $W$ has a basis, and $\dim W \le n$.

**Proof:** Let $w_1 \in W$, $w_1 \neq O$. If $\{w_1\}$ is not maximal in $W$, find $w_2 \in W$ such that $w_1, w_2$ are independent. Continue. By Theorem 3.1, this process stops at some $m \le n$. The resulting $\{w_1, \dots, w_m\}$ is a maximal independent set in $W$, hence a basis by Theorem 3.3.

## 1.9 Sums and Direct Sums

### 1.9.1 Sum of Subspaces

Let $U, W$ be subspaces of $V$. The **sum** $U + W$ is the set of all $u + w$ with $u \in U$ and $w \in W$.

$U + W$ is a subspace of $V$:
- $(u_1 + w_1) + (u_2 + w_2) = (u_1 + u_2) + (w_1 + w_2) \in U + W$
- $c(u + w) = cu + cw \in U + W$
- $O + O \in U + W$

### 1.9.2 Direct Sum

$V$ is a **direct sum** of $U$ and $W$ if for every $v \in V$ there exist **unique** $u \in U$ and $w \in W$ such that $v = u + w$.

**Notation:** $V = U \oplus W$.

**Theorem 4.1.** Let $U, W$ be subspaces. If $U + W = V$ and $U \cap W = \{O\}$, then $V = U \oplus W$.

**Proof:** Given $v \in V$, there exist $u \in U$, $w \in W$ with $v = u + w$ (since $U + W = V$). For uniqueness: suppose $v = u' + w'$. Then $u - u' = w' - w$. Since $u - u' \in U$ and $w' - w \in W$, both belong to $U \cap W = \{O\}$. Hence $u = u'$ and $w = w'$.

### 1.9.3 Existence of Complementary Subspaces

**Theorem 4.2.** Let $V$ be finite dimensional and $W$ be a subspace. Then there exists a subspace $U$ such that $V = W \oplus U$.

**Proof:** Select a basis $\{v_1, \dots, v_r\}$ of $W$, extend it to a basis $\{v_1, \dots, v_n\}$ of $V$ (Corollary 3.6). Let $U$ be generated by $\{v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n\}$. Then $V = W \oplus U$.

> **Note:** Given $W$, there are usually many such $U$.

### 1.9.4 Dimension of Direct Sums

**Theorem 4.3.** If $V = U \oplus W$, then:

$$
\dim V = \dim U + \dim W.
$$

**Proof:** Let $\{u_1, \dots, u_r\}$ be a basis of $U$ and $\{w_1, \dots, w_s\}$ a basis of $W$. Every $v \in V$ has a unique expression:

$$
v = x_1 u_1 + \cdots + x_r u_r + y_1 w_1 + \cdots + y_s w_s.
$$

Thus $\{u_1, \dots, u_r, w_1, \dots, w_s\}$ is a basis of $V$, so $\dim V = r + s$.

### 1.9.5 Direct Product

Let $U, W$ be arbitrary vector spaces over $K$. The **direct product** $U \times W$ is the set of all pairs $(u, w)$ with $u \in U$, $w \in W$.

Operations (componentwise):
- $(u_1, w_1) + (u_2, w_2) = (u_1 + u_2, w_1 + w_2)$
- $c(u, w) = (cu, cw)$

Then $U \times W$ is a vector space.

**Example:** $K^n = K^r \times K^s$ when $n = r + s$.

$$
\dim(U \times W) = \dim U + \dim W.
$$

### 1.9.6 Direct Sum of Several Subspaces

Let $V_1, \dots, V_n$ be subspaces of $V$. $V$ is the **direct sum**:

$$
V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n V_i = V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_n
$$

if every $v \in V$ has a **unique** expression:

$$
v = v_1 + \cdots + v_n \quad \text{with } v_i \in V_i.
$$

### 1.9.7 Direct Product of Several Spaces

Let $W_1, \dots, W_n$ be vector spaces. Their **direct product**:

$$
\prod_{i=1}^n W_i = W_1 \times \cdots \times W_n
$$

is the set of $n$-tuples $(w_1, \dots, w_n)$ with $w_i \in W_i$. Addition and scalar multiplication are defined componentwise.
