# 11. Polynomials and Primary Decomposition

## 11.1 The Euclidean Algorithm

We have already defined polynomials and their degree in Chapter 9. In this chapter, we deal with the other standard properties of polynomials. The basic one is the Euclidean algorithm, or long division.

**Theorem 1.1.** Let $f, g$ be polynomials over the field $K$, i.e. polynomials in $K[t]$, and assume $\deg g \geq 0$. Then there exist polynomials $q, r$ in $K[t]$ such that

$$
f(t) = q(t)g(t) + r(t),
$$

and $\deg r < \deg g$. The polynomials $q, r$ are uniquely determined by these conditions.

**Proof.** Let $m = \deg g \geq 0$. Write

$$
f(t) = a_n t^n + \cdots + a_0, \quad g(t) = b_m t^m + \cdots + b_0,
$$

with $b_m \neq 0$. If $n < m$, let $q = 0$ and $r = f$. If $n \geq m$, let

$$
f_1(t) = f(t) - a_n b_m^{-1} t^{n-m} g(t).
$$

Then $\deg f_1 < \deg f$. Continuing inductively, we find polynomials $q_1, r$ such that $f_1 = q_1 g + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(t) &= a_n b_m^{-1} t^{n-m} g(t) + f_1(t) \\
&= a_n b_m^{-1} t^{n-m} g(t) + q_1(t)g(t) + r(t) \\
&= \bigl(a_n b_m^{-1} t^{n-m} + q_1\bigr) g(t) + r(t),
\end{aligned}
$$

which expresses $f$ in the desired form.

For uniqueness, suppose $f = q_1 g + r_1 = q_2 g + r_2$ with $\deg r_1, \deg r_2 < \deg g$. Then:

$$
(q_1 - q_2) g = r_2 - r_1.
$$

The degree of the left-hand side is either $\geq \deg g$ or the left-hand side is $0$. The degree of the right-hand side is either $< \deg g$ or it is $0$. Hence the only possibility is that both sides are $0$, whence $q_1 = q_2$ and $r_1 = r_2$. ∎

**Corollary 1.2 (Factor Theorem).** Let $f$ be a non-zero polynomial in $K[t]$. Let $\alpha \in K$ be such that $f(\alpha) = 0$. Then there exists a polynomial $q(t)$ in $K[t]$ such that

$$
f(t) = (t - \alpha) q(t).
$$

**Proof.** By Theorem 1.1, we can write $f(t) = q(t)(t - \alpha) + r(t)$ where $\deg r < \deg(t - \alpha) = 1$. Hence $r$ is constant. Since $0 = f(\alpha) = q(\alpha)(\alpha - \alpha) + r(\alpha) = r(\alpha)$, it follows that $r = 0$. ∎

**Corollary 1.3.** Let $K$ be a field such that every non-constant polynomial in $K[t]$ has a root in $K$. Let $f$ be such a polynomial. Then there exist elements $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n \in K$ and $c \in K$ such that

$$
f(t) = c (t - \alpha_1) \cdots (t - \alpha_n).
$$

**Proof.** In Corollary 1.2, observe that $\deg q = \deg f - 1$. Let $\alpha = \alpha_1$. By assumption, if $q$ is not constant, we can find a root $\alpha_2$ of $q$, and thus write $f(t) = q_2(t)(t - \alpha_1)(t - \alpha_2)$. Proceeding inductively, we keep going until $q_n$ is constant. ∎

> **Note.** Assuming that the complex numbers satisfy the hypothesis of Corollary 1.3, we have proved the existence of a factorization of a polynomial over $\mathbb{C}$ into factors of degree 1. The uniqueness will be proved in the next section.

**Corollary 1.4.** Let $f$ be a polynomial of degree $n$ in $K[t]$. There are at most $n$ roots of $f$ in $K$.

**Proof.** Otherwise, if $m > n$ and $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_m$ are distinct roots of $f$ in $K$, then $f(t) = (t - \alpha_1) \cdots (t - \alpha_m) g(t)$ for some polynomial $g$, whence $\deg f \geq m$, a contradiction. ∎

## 11.2 Greatest Common Divisor

We shall define a notion which bears to the set of polynomials $K[t]$ the same relation as a subspace bears to a vector space.

**Definition (Ideal).** By an **ideal** of $K[t]$, or a **polynomial ideal**, we mean a subset $J$ of $K[t]$ satisfying:
- The zero polynomial is in $J$;
- If $f, g$ are in $J$, then $f + g$ is in $J$;
- If $f$ is in $J$ and $g$ is an arbitrary polynomial in $K[t]$, then $gf$ is in $J$.

From the last condition, if $c \in K$ and $f \in J$, then $cf$ is also in $J$. Thus an ideal may be viewed as a vector space over $K$, but it is more than that since it is closed under multiplication by arbitrary elements of $K[t]$.

**Example 1.** Let $f_1, \dots, f_n$ be polynomials in $K[t]$. Let $J$ be the set of all polynomials which can be written as

$$
g = g_1 f_1 + \cdots + g_n f_n
$$

with $g_i \in K[t]$. Then $J$ is an ideal. Indeed, it is closed under addition, contains $0$, and if $f \in K[t]$ then $fg = (fg_1)f_1 + \cdots + (fg_n)f_n$ also lies in $J$.

The ideal $J$ in Example 1 is said to be **generated by** $f_1, \dots, f_n$, and we say that $f_1, \dots, f_n$ are a **set of generators**.

**Example 2.** The single element $0$ is an ideal. Also, $K[t]$ itself is an ideal. We note that $1$ is a generator for $K[t]$, which is called the **unit ideal**.

**Example 3.** Consider the ideal generated by $t - 1$ and $t - 2$. Since $(t - 1) - (t - 2) = 1$ lies in it, this ideal is the unit ideal. Thus several generators may still admit a single generator.

**Theorem 2.1.** Let $J$ be an ideal of $K[t]$. Then there exists a polynomial $g$ which is a generator of $J$.

**Proof.** If $J$ is the zero ideal, we are done. Otherwise, let $g$ be a non-zero polynomial in $J$ of smallest degree. We assert that $g$ generates $J$. Let $f$ be any element of $J$. By the Euclidean algorithm, we can write $f = qg + r$ with $\deg r < \deg g$. Then $r = f - qg$ lies in $J$. Since $\deg r < \deg g$, minimality forces $r = 0$. Hence $f = qg$, and $g$ is a generator. ∎

> **Remark.** Let $g_1$ be a non-zero generator for an ideal $J$, and let $g_2$ also be a generator. Then $g_1 = qg_2$, so $\deg g_1 = \deg q + \deg g_2$. By symmetry $\deg g_2 = \deg g_1$, hence $q$ is constant and $g_1 = cg_2$. By rescaling we can always find a unique monic generator (leading coefficient $= 1$) for any non-zero ideal.

**Definition (Divides).** Let $f, g$ be non-zero polynomials. We say that $g$ **divides** $f$, and write $g \mid f$, if there exists a polynomial $q$ such that $f = gq$.

**Definition (Greatest common divisor).** Let $f_1, f_2$ be non-zero polynomials. A **greatest common divisor** of $f_1, f_2$ is a polynomial $g$ such that $g$ divides $f_1$ and $f_2$, and furthermore, if $h$ divides both $f_1$ and $f_2$, then $h$ divides $g$.

**Theorem 2.2.** Let $f_1, f_2$ be non-zero polynomials in $K[t]$. Let $g$ be a generator for the ideal generated by $f_1, f_2$. Then $g$ is a greatest common divisor of $f_1$ and $f_2$.

**Proof.** Since $f_1$ lies in the ideal generated by $f_1, f_2$, we have $f_1 = q_1 g$, so $g \mid f_1$; similarly $g \mid f_2$. Let $h$ divide both $f_1$ and $f_2$, so $f_1 = h_1 h$ and $f_2 = h_2 h$. Since $g$ is in the ideal, there exist $g_1, g_2$ such that $g = g_1 f_1 + g_2 f_2 = (g_1 h_1 + g_2 h_2)h$. Hence $h \mid g$. ∎

> **Remark 1.** The greatest common divisor is determined up to a non-zero constant multiple. If we select a GCD with leading coefficient $1$, then it is uniquely determined.

> **Remark 2.** Exactly the same proof applies for more than two polynomials. If $f_1, \dots, f_n$ are non-zero and $g$ generates the ideal $(f_1, \dots, f_n)$, then $g$ is a GCD of $f_1, \dots, f_n$.

**Definition (Relatively prime).** Polynomials $f_1, \dots, f_n$ whose greatest common divisor is $1$ are said to be **relatively prime**.

## 11.3 Unique Factorization

### 11.3.1 Irreducible Polynomials

**Definition (Irreducible).** A polynomial $p$ in $K[t]$ is said to be **irreducible** (over $K$) if it is of degree $\geq 1$, and if, given a factorization $p = fg$ with $f, g \in K[t]$, then $\deg f = 0$ or $\deg g = 0$ (i.e. one of $f, g$ is constant). Thus, up to a non-zero constant factor, the only divisors of $p$ are $p$ itself and $1$.

**Example 1.** The only irreducible polynomials over $\mathbb{C}$ are the polynomials of degree $1$, i.e. non-zero constant multiples of polynomials of type $t - \alpha$ with $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$.

**Example 2.** The polynomial $t^2 + 1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$.

### 11.3.2 Unique Factorization Theorem

**Theorem 3.1.** Every polynomial in $K[t]$ of degree $\geq 1$ can be expressed as a product $p_1 \cdots p_m$ of irreducible polynomials. In such a product, the polynomials $p_1, \dots, p_m$ are uniquely determined, up to a rearrangement, and up to non-zero constant factors.

**Proof (Existence).** Let $f \in K[t]$ have degree $\geq 1$. If $f$ is irreducible, we are done. Otherwise, we can write $f = gh$ where $\deg g < \deg f$ and $\deg h < \deg f$. If $g, h$ are irreducible, we are done; otherwise we continue factoring into polynomials of lower degree. This process must terminate (formally by induction on degree), yielding a factorization for $f$.

**Proof (Uniqueness).** We need a lemma.

**Lemma 3.2.** Let $p$ be irreducible in $K[t]$. Let $f, g \in K[t]$ be non-zero polynomials, and assume $p$ divides $fg$. Then $p$ divides $f$ or $p$ divides $g$.

**Proof.** Assume $p$ does not divide $f$. Then $\gcd(p, f) = 1$, and by Theorem 2.2 there exist polynomials $h_1, h_2 \in K[t]$ such that $1 = h_1 p + h_2 f$. Multiplying by $g$:

$$
g = gh_1 p + h_2 fg = gh_1 p + h_2 p h_3 = (gh_1 + h_2 h_3) p,
$$

where $fg = p h_3$. Thus $p$ divides $g$. ∎

Now apply the lemma when $p$ divides a product of irreducible polynomials $q_1 \cdots q_s$. Then $p$ divides $q_1$ or $p$ divides $q_2 \cdots q_s$. In the former case $p = c q_1$ for some constant $c$; in the latter case we proceed inductively. We conclude that in any case, there exists some $i$ such that $p$ and $q_i$ differ by a constant factor.

Suppose $p_1 \cdots p_r = q_1 \cdots q_s$ are two products of irreducible polynomials. After renumbering the $q_i$, we may assume $p_1 = c_1 q_1$. Cancelling $q_1$ and repeating inductively, we conclude that $r = s$ and there exist constants $c_i$ such that $p_i = c_i q_i$ for all $i$ (after a suitable permutation). ∎

**Corollary 3.3.** Let $f$ be a polynomial in $K[t]$ of degree $\geq 1$. Then $f$ has a factorization $f = c p_1 \cdots p_s$, where $p_1, \dots, p_s$ are irreducible polynomials with leading coefficient $1$, uniquely determined up to a permutation.

**Corollary 3.4.** Let $f$ be a polynomial in $\mathbb{C}[t]$ of degree $\geq 1$. Then $f$ has a factorization

$$
f(t) = c (t - \alpha_1) \cdots (t - \alpha_n),
$$

with $\alpha_i \in \mathbb{C}$ and $c \in \mathbb{C}$. The factors $t - \alpha_i$ are uniquely determined up to a permutation.

### 11.3.3 Multiplicity and the Derivative

We shall deal mostly with polynomials having leading coefficient $1$.

**Definition (Monic).** A polynomial with leading coefficient $1$ is called **monic**.

Let $f$ be a monic polynomial of degree $\geq 1$. Let $p_1, \dots, p_r$ be the distinct irreducible monic polynomials occurring in its factorization. Then we can express $f$ as a product

$$
f = p_1^{i_1} \cdots p_r^{i_r},
$$

where $i_1, \dots, i_r$ are positive integers uniquely determined by $p_1, \dots, p_r$. This is called a **normalized factorization** for $f$. In particular, over $\mathbb{C}$:

$$
f(t) = (t - \alpha_1)^{i_1} \cdots (t - \alpha_r)^{i_r}.
$$

**Definition (Multiplicity).** If $p$ is irreducible and $f = p^m g$ where $p$ does not divide $g$ and $m \geq 0$, we say that $m$ is the **multiplicity** of $p$ in $f$, denoted $\operatorname{ord}_p f$. If $\alpha$ is a root of $f$ and $f(t) = (t - \alpha)^m g(t)$ with $g(\alpha) \neq 0$, then $m$ is the **multiplicity of $\alpha$ in $f$**.

**Definition (Formal derivative).** Let $f(t) = a_n t^n + \cdots + a_0$. Its **(formal) derivative** is defined by

$$
Df(t) = f'(t) = n a_n t^{n-1} + (n-1) a_{n-1} t^{n-2} + \cdots + a_1.
$$

The following properties hold (proofs left as exercises):

| Property | Formula |
|----------|---------|
| **Sum rule** | $(f + g)' = f' + g'$ |
| **Product rule** | $(fg)' = f'g + fg'$ |
| **Constant multiple** | $(cf)' = c f'$ ($c$ constant) |

> **Exercise.** Let $\alpha$ be a root of $f$ with $\deg f \geq 1$. Show that the multiplicity of $\alpha$ in $f$ is $> 1$ if and only if $f'(\alpha) = 0$. Hence if $f'(\alpha) \neq 0$, the multiplicity of $\alpha$ is $1$.

## 11.4 Application to the Decomposition of a Vector Space

Let $V$ be a vector space over the field $K$, and let $A: V \to V$ be an operator. A subspace $W$ of $V$ is called an **invariant subspace under $A$** if $Aw$ lies in $W$ for each $w \in W$, i.e. if $AW$ is contained in $W$.

**Example 1.** Let $v_1$ be a non-zero eigenvector of $A$, and let $V_1$ be the $1$-dimensional space generated by $v_1$. Then $V_1$ is an invariant subspace under $A$.

**Example 2.** Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$, and let $V_\lambda$ be the subspace of $V$ consisting of all $v \in V$ such that $Av = \lambda v$. Then $V_\lambda$ is an invariant subspace under $A$, called the **eigenspace** of $\lambda$.

**Example 3.** Let $f(t) \in K[t]$ be a polynomial, and let $W$ be the kernel of $f(A)$. Then $W$ is an invariant subspace under $A$.

**Proof.** Suppose $f(A)w = O$. Since $tf(t) = f(t)t$, we get $Af(A) = f(A)A$, whence

$$
f(A)(Aw) = f(A)Aw = Af(A)w = O.
$$

Thus $Aw$ is also in the kernel of $f(A)$. ∎

> **Remark.** For any two polynomials $f, g$ we have $f(A)g(A) = g(A)f(A)$ because $f(t)g(t) = g(t)f(t)$.

We now describe how the factorization of a polynomial into two factors whose greatest common divisor is $1$ gives rise to a decomposition of $V$ into a direct sum of invariant subspaces.

**Theorem 4.1.** Let $f \in K[t]$ be a polynomial, and suppose that $f = f_1 f_2$, where $f_1, f_2$ are polynomials of degree $\geq 1$ and greatest common divisor equal to $1$. Let $A: V \to V$ be an operator. Assume that $f(A) = O$. Let

$$
W_1 = \ker f_1(A) \quad \text{and} \quad W_2 = \ker f_2(A).
$$

Then $V$ is the direct sum of $W_1$ and $W_2$.

**Proof.** Since $\gcd(f_1, f_2) = 1$, there exist polynomials $g_1, g_2$ such that $g_1(t)f_1(t) + g_2(t)f_2(t) = 1$. Substituting $A$:

$$
g_1(A)f_1(A) + g_2(A)f_2(A) = I. \tag{*}
$$

For any $v \in V$:

$$
v = g_1(A)f_1(A)v + g_2(A)f_2(A)v.
$$

The first term lies in $W_2$ because $f_2(A)g_1(A)f_1(A)v = g_1(A)f_1(A)f_2(A)v = g_1(A)f(A)v = O$. Similarly the second term lies in $W_1$. Thus $V = W_1 + W_2$.

To show the sum is direct, suppose $v = w_1 + w_2$ with $w_1 \in W_1$ and $w_2 \in W_2$. Applying $g_1(A)f_1(A)$ to this sum:

$$
g_1(A)f_1(A)v = g_1(A)f_1(A)w_2,
$$

since $f_1(A)w_1 = O$. Applying $(*)$ to $w_2$ itself gives $w_2 = g_1(A)f_1(A)w_2$ because $f_2(A)w_2 = O$. Hence $w_2 = g_1(A)f_1(A)v$, uniquely determined. Similarly $w_1 = g_2(A)f_2(A)v$ is uniquely determined, so the sum is direct. ∎

**Theorem 4.2.** Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$, and let $A: V \to V$ be an operator. Let $P(t)$ be a polynomial such that $P(A) = O$, and let

$$
P(t) = (t - \alpha_1)^{m_1} \cdots (t - \alpha_r)^{m_r}
$$

be its factorization, with $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_r$ the distinct roots. Let $W_i$ be the kernel of $(A - \alpha_i I)^{m_i}$. Then $V$ is the direct sum of the subspaces $W_1, \dots, W_r$.

**Proof.** By induction, splitting off the factors one by one. Let $W_1 = \ker(A - \alpha_1 I)^{m_1}$ and $W = \ker(A - \alpha_2 I)^{m_2} \cdots (A - \alpha_r I)^{m_r}$. By Theorem 4.1 we obtain $V = W_1 \oplus W$. Inductively, $W = W_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus W_r$ where $W_j = \ker(A - \alpha_j I)^{m_j}$ in $W$. One then verifies that $W_j$ is also the kernel of $(A - \alpha_j I)^{m_j}$ in $V$. ∎

### 11.4.1 Application to Differential Equations

**Example 4.** Let $V$ be the space of (infinitely differentiable) solutions of the differential equation

$$
D^n f + a_{n-1} D^{n-1} f + \cdots + a_0 f = 0,
$$

with constant complex coefficients $a_i$.

**Theorem 4.3.** Let $P(t) = t^n + a_{n-1} t^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$. Factor $P(t)$ as

$$
P(t) = (t - \alpha_1)^{m_1} \cdots (t - \alpha_r)^{m_r}.
$$

Then $V$ is the direct sum of the spaces of solutions of the differential equations

$$
(D - \alpha_i I)^{m_i} f = 0, \quad i = 1, \dots, r.
$$

**Proof.** This is a direct application of Theorem 4.2. ∎

Thus the study of the original differential equation is reduced to the study of the much simpler equation $(D - \alpha I)^m f = 0$.

**Theorem 4.4.** Let $\alpha$ be a complex number. Let $W$ be the space of solutions of the differential equation $(D - \alpha I)^m f = 0$. Then $W$ is the space generated by the functions

$$
e^{\alpha t}, \; t e^{\alpha t}, \; \dots, \; t^{m-1} e^{\alpha t},
$$

and these functions form a basis for this space, which therefore has dimension $m$.

**Proof.** For any complex $\alpha$ we have $(D - \alpha I)^m f = e^{\alpha t} D^m(e^{-\alpha t} f)$ (proved by induction). Consequently, $f$ lies in the kernel of $(D - \alpha I)^m$ if and only if $D^m(e^{-\alpha t} f) = 0$. The only functions whose $m$-th derivative is $0$ are polynomials of degree $\leq m-1$. Hence the solution space is generated by $e^{\alpha t}, t e^{\alpha t}, \dots, t^{m-1} e^{\alpha t}$.

For linear independence, suppose $c_0 e^{\alpha t} + c_1 t e^{\alpha t} + \cdots + c_{m-1} t^{m-1} e^{\alpha t} = 0$. Let $Q(t) = c_0 + c_1 t + \cdots + c_{m-1} t^{m-1}$. Then $Q(t) e^{\alpha t} = 0$ for all $t$. Since $e^{\alpha t} \neq 0$, we have $Q(t) = 0$ for all $t$, whence $c_i = 0$ for all $i$. ∎

## 11.5 Schur's Lemma

Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$, and let $S$ be a set of operators of $V$. Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$. We shall say that $W$ is an **$S$-invariant subspace** if $BW$ is contained in $W$ for all $B$ in $S$. We shall say that $V$ is a **simple $S$-space** if $V \neq \{O\}$ and if the only $S$-invariant subspaces are $V$ itself and the zero subspace.

**Remark 1.** Let $A: V \to V$ be an operator such that $AB = BA$ for all $B \in S$. Then the image and kernel of $A$ are $S$-invariant subspaces of $V$.

**Proof.** Let $w$ be in the image of $A$, say $w = Av$ with $v \in V$. Then $Bw = BAv = ABv$, so $Bw$ is also in the image of $A$. Let $u$ be in the kernel of $A$. Then $ABu = BAu = O$, so $Bu$ is also in the kernel. ∎

**Remark 2.** Let $S$ be as above, and let $A: V \to V$ be an operator. Assume $AB = BA$ for all $B \in S$. If $f$ is a polynomial in $K[t]$, then $f(A)B = Bf(A)$ for all $B \in S$.

**Theorem 5.1 (Schur's Lemma).** Let $V$ be a vector space over $K$, and let $S$ be a set of operators of $V$. Assume that $V$ is a simple $S$-space. Let $A: V \to V$ be a linear map such that $AB = BA$ for all $B$ in $S$. Then either $A$ is invertible or $A$ is the zero map.

**Proof.** Assume $A \neq O$. By Remark 1, the kernel of $A$ is $\{O\}$ (it cannot be $V$ since $A \neq O$), and its image is all of $V$ (it cannot be $\{O\}$ since $A \neq O$). Hence $A$ is invertible. ∎

**Theorem 5.2.** Let $V$ be a finite dimensional vector space over the complex numbers. Let $S$ be a set of operators of $V$, and assume that $V$ is a simple $S$-space. Let $A: V \to V$ be a linear map such that $AB = BA$ for all $B$ in $S$. Then there exists a number $\lambda$ such that $A = \lambda I$.

**Proof.** Let $J$ be the ideal of polynomials $f$ in $\mathbb{C}[t]$ such that $f(A) = O$. Let $g$ be a generator for this ideal, with leading coefficient $1$. Then $g \neq 0$. We contend that $g$ is irreducible. Otherwise, we can write $g = h_1 h_2$ with polynomials $h_1, h_2$ of degrees $< \deg g$. Consequently $h_1(A) \neq O$. By Theorem 5.1 and Remarks 1, 2 we conclude that $h_1(A)$ is invertible. Similarly, $h_2(A)$ is invertible. Hence $h_1(A)h_2(A)$ is invertible, an impossibility since $h_1(A)h_2(A) = g(A) = O$. This proves that $g$ must be irreducible.

But the only irreducible polynomials over the complex numbers are of degree $1$, and hence $g(t) = t - \lambda$ for some $\lambda \in \mathbb{C}$. Since $g(A) = O$, we conclude that $A - \lambda I = O$, whence $A = \lambda I$. ∎

## 11.6 The Jordan Normal Form

In Chapter 10, §1 we proved that a linear map over the complex numbers can always be triangulated. This result suffices for many applications, but it is possible to improve it and find a basis such that the matrix of the linear map has an exceptionally simple triangular form. We do this now, using the primary decomposition.

### 11.6.1 Cyclic Vectors and Jordan Bases

We first consider a special case, which turns out to be rather typical afterwards. Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{C}$, let $A: V \to V$ be a linear map, let $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ and let $v \in V$, $v \neq O$.

**Definition ($(A-\alpha I)$-cyclic).** We shall say that $v$ is **$(A-\alpha I)$-cyclic** if there exists an integer $r \geq 1$ such that $(A-\alpha I)^r v = O$. The smallest positive integer $r$ having this property is called a **period** of $v$ relative to $A-\alpha I$. If $r$ is such a period, then $(A-\alpha I)^k v \neq O$ for any integer $k$ with $0 \leq k < r$.

**Lemma 6.1.** If $v \neq O$ is $(A-\alpha I)$-cyclic, with period $r$, then the elements

$$
v, \quad (A-\alpha I)v, \quad \dots, \quad (A-\alpha I)^{r-1}v
$$

are linearly independent.

**Proof.** Let $B = A - \alpha I$. A relation of linear dependence can be written $f(B)v = O$, where $f$ is a non-zero polynomial of degree $\leq r-1$, say $f(t) = c_0 + c_1 t + \cdots + c_s t^s$ with $s \leq r-1$. We also have $B^r v = O$ by hypothesis. Let $g(t) = t^r$. If $h = \gcd(f, g)$, then $h = f_1 f + g_1 g$ for some polynomials $f_1, g_1$, and thus $h(B)v = f_1(B)f(B)v + g_1(B)g(B)v = O$. But $h(t)$ divides $t^r$ and has degree $\leq r-1$, so $h(t) = t^d$ with $d < r$. This contradicts the hypothesis that $r$ is a period of $v$. ∎

**Definition (Cyclic space).** The vector space $V$ will be called **cyclic** if there exists some number $\alpha$ and an element $v \in V$ which is $(A-\alpha I)$-cyclic and $v, Av, \dots, A^{r-1}v$ generate $V$.

If this is the case, Lemma 6.1 implies that

$$
\bigl\{(A-\alpha I)^{r-1}v, \dots, (A-\alpha I)v, v\bigr\} \tag{*}
$$

is a basis for $V$. With respect to this basis, for each $k$ we have

$$
A(A-\alpha I)^k v = (A-\alpha I)^{k+1}v + \alpha(A-\alpha I)^k v.
$$

Hence the matrix of $A$ with respect to this basis is the triangular matrix

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\alpha & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
0 & \alpha & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
\vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & \alpha & 1 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \alpha
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

This matrix has $\alpha$ on the diagonal, $1$ above the diagonal, and $0$ everywhere else. The vector $(A-\alpha I)^{r-1}v$ is an eigenvector for $A$ with eigenvalue $\alpha$.

The basis $(*)$ is called a **Jordan basis** for $V$ with respect to $A$.

### 11.6.2 Existence of the Jordan Normal Form

Suppose that $V$ is expressed as a direct sum of $A$-invariant subspaces,

$$
V = V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_m,
$$

and suppose that each $V_i$ is cyclic. If we select a Jordan basis for each $V_i$, then the union of these bases forms a basis for $V$, again called a **Jordan basis for $V$ with respect to $A$**. With respect to this basis, the matrix for $A$ splits into blocks. In each block we have an eigenvalue $\alpha_i$ on the diagonal, $1$ above the diagonal, and $0$ everywhere else. This matrix is called the **Jordan normal form** for $A$.

Our main theorem in this section is that this normal form can always be achieved.

**Theorem 6.2.** Let $V$ be a finite dimensional space over the complex numbers, and $V \neq \{O\}$. Let $A: V \to V$ be an operator. Then $V$ can be expressed as a direct sum of $A$-invariant cyclic subspaces.

**Proof.** By Theorem 4.2 we may assume without loss of generality that there exists a number $\alpha$ and an integer $r \geq 1$ such that $(A-\alpha I)^r = O$. Let $B = A - \alpha I$. Then $B^r = O$. We assume that $r$ is the smallest such integer; then $B^{r-1} \neq O$. The subspace $BV$ is not equal to $V$ because its dimension is strictly smaller (for instance, there exists $w \in V$ with $B^{r-1}w \neq O$; let $v = B^{r-1}w$, then $Bv = O$).

By induction, we may write $BV$ as a direct sum of $A$-invariant (or $B$-invariant) cyclic subspaces,

$$
BV = W_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus W_m,
$$

such that $W_i$ has a basis consisting of elements $B^k w_i$ for some cyclic vector $w_i \in W_i$ of period $r_i$. Let $v_i \in V$ be such that $Bv_i = w_i$. Then each $v_i$ is a cyclic vector, because if $B^{r_i} w_i = O$, then $B^{r_i+1} v_i = O$.

Let $V_i$ be the subspace of $V$ generated by $B^k v_i$ for $k = 0, \dots, r_i$. We contend that the subspace $V' = V_1 + \cdots + V_m$ is a direct sum. Suppose

$$
f_1(B)v_1 + \cdots + f_m(B)v_m = O, \tag{1}
$$

where each $f_i$ is a polynomial of degree $\leq r_i$. Applying $B$ and noting that $Bf_i(B) = f_i(B)B$ gives $f_1(B)w_1 + \cdots + f_m(B)w_m = O$. Since $W_1 + \cdots + W_m$ is a direct sum, $f_i(B)w_i = O$ for all $i$. Therefore $t^{r_i}$ divides $f_i(t)$; in particular $t$ divides $f_i(t)$, so $f_i(t) = g_i(t)t$ and $f_i(B) = g_i(B)B$. It follows from (1) that $g_1(B)w_1 + \cdots + g_m(B)w_m = O$. Again $t^{r_i}$ divides $g_i(t)$, whence $t^{r_i+1}$ divides $f_i(t)$, and therefore $f_i(B)v_i = O$. This proves that $V'$ is a direct sum.

From the construction, $BV' = BV$. We shall conclude that $V = V' + \ker B$. Indeed, let $v \in V$. Then $Bv = Bv'$ for some $v' \in V'$, hence $B(v-v') = O$. Thus $v = v' + (v-v')$, proving $V = V' + \ker B$. Of course this sum is not necessarily direct. However, let $\mathscr{B}'$ be a Jordan basis of $V'$. We can extend $\mathscr{B}'$ to a basis of $V$ by using elements of $\ker B$. If $\{u_1, \dots, u_s\}$ is a basis of $\ker B$, then for suitable indices we obtain a basis of $V$. Each $u_j$ satisfies $Bu_j = O$, whence $u_j$ is an eigenvector for $A$; the one-dimensional space generated by $u_j$ is $A$-invariant and cyclic. Denote this subspace by $U_j$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V &= V' \oplus U_{j_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus U_{j_l} \\
&= V_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus V_m \oplus U_{j_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus U_{j_l},
\end{aligned}
$$

giving the desired expression of $V$ as a direct sum of cyclic subspaces. ∎
